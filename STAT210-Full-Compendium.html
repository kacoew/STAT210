Everything in this course is one idea, extended four times. The idea: split the variation you observe into a part explained by something and a part that is not, then compare the two.

The load-bearing idea for the whole course

Every ANOVA table you will ever see is the same four columns: a sum of squares, its degrees of freedom, their ratio the mean square, and an F formed by dividing one mean square by another.

Chapter 1 · Lecture 1Probability and the four distributions

Lecture 1 is a toolbox. Nothing in it is interesting alone; every piece is there because ANOVA needs it later. What you are examined on is recognising which distribution a quantity has, and reading values off a table.

What the exam actually asks from lecture 1

On the exam — this is reliable

Why t has fatter tails than the normal

In Z = (ȳ − μ) ⁄ (σ/√n) the bottom is a fixed constant. In T = (ȳ − μ) ⁄ (s/√n) the bottom is itself estimated from the data, so it wobbles too.

Two sources of wobble instead of one means extreme values happen more often — fatter tails, so bigger critical values. With few degrees of freedom the estimate of σ is poor and the tails are very fat (t0.025,3 = 3.18); with many it is almost the normal (t0.025,36 = 2.03 against z = 1.96).

Rules for combining random variables

These four lines look trivial. Every standard error in the entire course is derived from them.

E(cY) = c·E(Y)     Var(cY) = c²·Var(Y)     Var(c) = 0
E(Σ ciYi) = Σ ciμi     Var(Σ ciYi) = Σ ci²σi²  (independent Y) Means scale with c. Variances scale with c squared. Double a variable and its variance quadruples.
The consequence that drives everything

Apply those rules to an average, where every ci = 1/n:

Var(Ȳ) = σ²n

Individual measurements never get less variable — σ² is a fact about the population. But the average of n of them is n times less variable. This single fact is behind: why more data gives more power, why confidence intervals shrink, why balance is optimal, and why the n appears in every expected mean square in lectures 5 and 8.

Chapter 2 · Lecture 2One-way ANOVA

The core machinery of the whole course. A single factor with a levels, n observations in each, assigned at random.

The model

yit = μ + τi + εit,    Στi = 0,    εit ~ iid N(0, σ²) Any measurement = the overall mean + the effect of its group + that individual's own randomness.
i = 1…a counts groups, t = 1…n counts replicates within a group.
On the exam — a guaranteed question type

You will be shown a model equation with the index ranges attached and asked which is correct, for example:

"Three brands, 8 observations each" → the right answer is yit = μ + τi + eit, i = 1,2,3; t = 1,…,8. The wrong options swap i and t, or drop the error term, or put μ on the left.

Rule: i always indexes the GROUP, t always indexes the REPLICATE. Check the ranges match the number of groups, not the sample size.

Why the constraint Σ τᵢ = 0 is needed

Count parameters: you have a group means to describe, but the model has 1 + a parameters. One too many. Add 5 to μ and subtract 5 from every τi and every fitted value is identical — the data can never distinguish them.

A constraint picks one solution. Sum-to-zero is the choice that makes μ mean "the overall level" and each τi mean "how far this group sits from that level".

The estimates

μ̂ = ȳ··     τ̂i = ȳi· − ȳ··     eit = yit − ȳi·     σ̂² = MSE Grand mean · group mean minus grand mean · observation minus its own group mean · the error mean square.

Splitting the variation

SST = SSTr + SSE Total variation = variation the groups explain + variation left over. Nothing lost, nothing double-counted.
The three sums of squares
NameFormulaIn words

SSTrn·Σi(ȳi· − ȳ··)²each group mean's distance from the overall mean, squared, times n
SSEΣi,t(yit − ȳi·)²every point's distance from its own group mean, squared and added
Trap — the missing n

SSTr has an n in front. Each group's squared deviation is counted once for every observation in that group, not once per group. Forget it and your answer is out by a factor of n — and the wrong value will usually be one of the five options.

Why squaring, and why the split is exact

Squaring: some deviations are positive and some negative. Added raw they cancel to zero. Squaring makes everything positive so you measure distance regardless of direction.

Why it splits exactly: write (yit − ȳ··) = (ȳi· − ȳ··) + (yit − ȳi·). Squaring normally leaves a cross-term, but summing it within a group gives Σt(yit − ȳi·) — deviations from their own mean, which is zero by construction. The cross-term dies for every group, so the identity is exact.

Degrees of freedom, mean squares, F

Why you cannot compare SS directly

SSE is built from more numbers than SSTr, so it would win on volume alone. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom turns a total into an average, and averages are comparable. Same reason you compare shopping bags by price per item, not total price.

Degrees of freedom — count of values genuinely free to vary

Why F sits near 1 when nothing is happening

MSE always estimates σ². When H₀ is true, MSTr also estimates σ², because then the group means differ only by sampling noise. A ratio of two estimates of the same thing sits near 1.

When treatments really differ, MSTr picks up the genuine spread on top of the noise, so it inflates and F grows. This is also why the test is upper-tailed only: real effects can only push F up.

On the exam — the missing-cell question

You are given a partial table and asked for a missing number. The whole thing is recoverable because everything is linked:

  • Given SS and df in a row → MS = SS/df
  • Given two of the three df → the third, since they add
  • Given F and one MS → the other MS
  • Given a and N → all three df immediately

Worked example from the 2025 paper. Three brands, 8 observations each, so a = 3, N = 24. Table shows SSTr = 33.403 on 2 df and SSE = 55.727 on 21 df. Asked for the estimate of σ:

MSE = 55.727 ⁄ 21 = 2.654, so σ̂ = √2.654 = 1.629

The distractors are 2.654 (forgot the square root) and 16.702 (used MSTr). Both are what you get from a single slip.

Chapter 3 · Lecture 3Inference — testing and confidence intervals

Lecture 3 appears to introduce many tests. It introduces one, applied to different quantities. Learn the one formula plus the table of standard errors and you have the whole lecture.

T = estimate − hypothesised valuestandard error of the estimate    with df = N − a Top: how big is the effect. Bottom: how much would this estimate bounce around if you repeated the study.
A 1 kg difference is huge if you measure to 0.1 kg, and nothing if you wobble by 5 kg. T is that comparison as one number.
The load-bearing idea

Every test in lectures 3 and 4 changes only the numerator. A group mean, the grand mean, a difference of two means, a contrast — same formula, same pooled MSE, same df = N − a.

Every standard error in the course — all use pooled MS_E, all with df = N − a
What you want to say something aboutEstimateStandard error

Why pooled MS_E rather than one group's own variance

You already assumed all groups share the same σ². Under that assumption every group's variance is an independent estimate of the same number, so averaging them all is far more stable than using one.

Concretely: one group of 2 piglets gives s² = 8 on 1 df, critical value 12.71. Pooling all three gives MSE = 6 on 3 df, critical value 3.18. Pooling buys precision; the equal-variance assumption is the price.

Confidence intervals

CI = estimate ± tα/2, N−a · se(estimate) The range of values that would not be rejected by a test at level α.
Duality — the fastest shortcut in the course

A two-sided test at level α and a (1−α) confidence interval are the same procedure wearing different clothes. Reject H₀: θ = θ₀ if and only if θ₀ lies outside the interval.

So if a question gives you an interval and asks whether some value is significantly different, you do not compute anything — you just look.

The interval for σ² — the one that behaves differently

SSEσ² ~ χ²N−a   ⟹   CI = [ SSEχ²1−α/2, N−a , SSEχ²α/2, N−a ] Asymmetric, because the chi-square distribution is lopsided.
The BIG chi-square value gives the SMALL end of the interval — because you are dividing by it.
On the exam — this appears almost every year

From the 2025 paper: SSE = 55.727, df = 21, and you are given χ²0.975,21 = 35.48 and χ²0.025,21 = 10.28. Find a 95% CI for σ — note, sigma, not sigma squared.

[ 55.727⁄35.48 , 55.727⁄10.28 ] = [1.571 , 5.421]  →  take square roots  →  [1.25 , 2.33]

Two traps built into the options: forgetting the square root (gives 1.63 to 5.81), and dividing by the wrong quantile first (bounds come out reversed).

The two errors, and power

H₀ true (no real effect)H₀ false (real effect)
You rejectType I error — probability α. A false alarm.Correct. Probability 1 − β = power
You do not rejectCorrect. Probability 1 − αType II error — probability β. A missed effect.

α is chosen by you, before the data. β is a consequence — of your α, your sample size, the true effect size and σ². You never observe β, which is why a non-significant result is so easy to over-read.

Power rises with: larger n (because Var(ȳ) = σ²/n), larger true effect, smaller σ². Tightening α lowers power. Only more data improves both errors at once.

On the exam — the sentence that scores

Never write "there is no effect" after a non-significant result. The defensible statement is "no significant evidence of an effect", and if n is small, add that power is low so a real effect could have been missed.

Multiple-choice options are frequently built around exactly this distinction: one option says "we conclude there is no difference" and another says "we cannot conclude there is a difference". The second is always the correct one.

Chapter 4 · Lecture 4Contrasts and post-hoc comparisons

The F-test says something is going on but not what. Lecture 4 is the toolkit for locating the effect without cheating.

Contrasts

Γ = Σi ci μi   subject to   Σi ci = 0 A specific comparison, written as a set of weights on the group means.
Why the weights must add to zero

Substitute μi = μ + τi:  Γ = μ·Σci + Σciτi. When Σci = 0 the first term vanishes and the grand mean drops out algebraically.

Practical meaning: add 100 to every group and the contrast is unchanged. That is what makes it a comparison rather than a level.

Orthogonality

Two contrasts are orthogonal — carrying non-overlapping information — when Σi c1ic2i = 0 (multiply element by element, add, get zero).

The partition theorem

With a groups you can build exactly a − 1 mutually orthogonal contrasts — matching dfTr = a − 1, which is not a coincidence. When you do, their sums of squares add up to SSTr exactly. The omnibus test is dissected into a−1 independent one-df questions with nothing left over.

Trap — rescaling

Multiplying a contrast by any constant changes Γ̂ and se(Γ̂), but never changes T₀, the p-value, or SSC. So (1, −1, 0, 0) and (2, −2, 0, 0) and (½, −½, 0, 0) all give the same test.

Why Tukey beats Bonferroni for pairwise work

Bonferroni treats the m comparisons as m unrelated problems and charges full price for each. Tukey knows something Bonferroni ignores: if the largest gap between any two means is not significant, no smaller gap can be either. So it only has to control that single largest gap. Solving the real problem instead of the worst imaginable one is what buys back the power.

On the trout data (a = 4, N = 40) the thresholds on the |T| scale come out: no correction 2.03 < Tukey 2.69 < Bonferroni 2.79 < Scheffé 2.93.

On the exam

The timetable states Tukey by hand is not required. What is required: knowing which correction suits which situation, that all three raise the bar, that Tukey is the least conservative of the three for pairwise comparisons, and being able to read a TukeyHSD output — if the confidence interval for a pair contains zero, that pair is not significantly different.

On the exam — the giveaway phrases

Read the question stem for these words: "a random sample of", "randomly selected", "randomly picked", "drawn from the population of". Any of these means random factor.

Real examples: "He took a random sample of 6 boars and a random sample of 4 swine farms" → both random. "The 6 rivers are randomly sampled from the population of Norwegian rivers" → river is random. "four different doses: 0, 5, 10, 15 g" → fixed, they were chosen.

In R output, look for r() around the variable name: lm(Size ~ r(Boar) + r(Farm)) means both are random.

The model and what follows from it

yit = μ + ti + εit,    ti ~ N(0, σ²τ),    εit ~ N(0, σ²),    independent Identical to the fixed model except the group effect is now itself a random draw.
Three consequences — all examined

Why two variance components appear

In the fixed model τi is a constant, and constants have no variance. Now it is a random variable, so it contributes its own variance.

Pick a cow at random from the country: two independent random things happened to her — her father was drawn from the bull population (contributing σ²τ), and she turned out however she turned out (contributing σ²). Both add to how unpredictable she is.

And two daughters of the same bull share his draw, which is exactly why they are correlated, with covariance σ²τ.

Expected mean squares and the estimates

E(MSE) = σ²      E(MSTr) = σ² + n·σ²τ MS_Tr is expected to be the noise plus an extra bit. If groups do not vary, the extra bit is zero and both estimate the same thing — so F sits near 1.

Setting the observed mean squares equal to their expected values and solving gives the method of moments estimators:

σ̂² = MSE      σ̂²τ = MSTr − MSEn Take how much bigger MS_Tr is than MS_E, and divide by the group size.
Trap — negative variance

Nothing stops MSE being bigger than MSTr, which returns a negative variance — impossible. It happens by bad luck when the true σ²τ is small. Report it as zero, or use REML, which cannot go negative. Any dataset with F < 1 gives a negative estimate.

The ICC

ICC = σ²τσ²τ + σ² Of all the variation, what fraction is due to the group? Always between 0 and 1.
It is also literally the correlation between two observations from the same group.

Fixed vs random — why df = a − 1 here

You built this standard error out of MSTr, so you inherit that mean square's degrees of freedom, a − 1. You never used MSE, so its df are irrelevant.

Montgomery 10th edition, page 107, says N − a. That is wrong, and your lecturer flags it on the slides — which makes it fair game for an exam question. Using the wrong df gives an interval about 25% too narrow.

The deeper reason: your 40 cows are not 40 independent pieces of information about the nation. They come from only 5 bulls. Your effective sample size is closer to 5 than to 40, and a − 1 reflects that.

Part 1 — diagnostics

Error vs residual — an examined distinction

Error εit = yit − (μ + τi) uses the true parameters. It can never be observed.

Residual ε̂it = yit − ȳi· uses the estimated group mean. This you can compute.

You diagnose the unobservable errors using the observable residuals as stand-ins. Residuals within each group always sum to exactly zero by construction — which is why you lose one df per group, giving N − a.

Transformations

Why a log transform fixes a funnel

A funnel — spread growing with the mean — says the underlying process is multiplicative, not additive: effects scale things rather than add to them.

Taking logs turns multiplication into addition. It compresses large values much more than small ones, so the wide right-hand end of the funnel is pulled in. This very often fixes both the variance problem and the skewness at once, because they had the same cause.

What changes when you take logs — this is examined

Fitting ln(yit) = μ + τi + εit is the same as saying the original data follow yit = eμ·eτi·eεit — a multiplicative model.

So on the original scale:

  • Back-transforming a mean gives a geometric mean, not an arithmetic one.
  • Differences on the log scale become ratios on the original scale. A log-scale difference of 1.269 means a ratio of e1.269 = 3.56 — group 3 has 3.56 times group 1, not 1.269 units more.
  • A CI [L, U] on the log scale becomes [eL, eU], a CI for the ratio.

Always state which scale you are reporting on.

Part 2 — the two-factor factorial design

Factor A has a levels, factor B has b levels. Crossed means every level of A appears with every level of B, giving a×b cells, with n replicates in each. Total N = a·b·n.

yijt = μ + τi + βj + (τβ)ij + εijt overall mean + main effect of A + main effect of B + interaction + noise
i = 1…a · j = 1…b · t = 1…n

All the constraints are sum-to-zero: Στi = 0, Σβj = 0, and the interaction sums to zero across every row and every column.

μ̂ = ȳ···  ·  τ̂i = ȳi·· − ȳ···  ·  β̂j = ȳ·j· − ȳ···
(τβ)iĵ = ȳij· − ȳi·· − ȳ·j· + ȳ··· The interaction estimate is the cell mean, minus what the two main effects alone would have predicted.
Trap — you need replication

With n = 1 (one observation per cell) you cannot estimate the interaction at all — there is nothing left over to estimate σ² with. The error df, ab(n−1), becomes zero.

Interaction — the most important new concept

What interaction means

The effect of factor A depends on which level of B you are in (and vice versa). "It depends on…" is the everyday phrase for it.

Without interaction, effects simply stack: if Clay is worth +2 and Fert2 is worth +3, then Clay-with-Fert2 is worth +5. With interaction, that combination is worth +5 + (τβ)12 — possibly much more (synergy) or much less (antagonism).

Test the interaction FIRST — always

Never look at the main-effect p-values before you know whether the interaction is significant. The interaction determines how the main effects may be interpreted.

If significant: keep it, and keep both main effects even if their p-values are huge. This is the principle of marginality. Report the cell means, because the main effects alone are misleading.

If not significant: you may drop it and fit the additive model y = μ + τi + βj + ε. Its (a−1)(b−1) df are pooled into the error, giving more error df, a smaller critical value, and more power for the main effects.

General rule: the df for any interaction is the product of the df of the factors in it. A×B×C has (a−1)(b−1)(c−1).

What a three-way interaction means

A two-way interaction says "the effect of A depends on B". A three-way interaction says "the way A and B interact is itself different at different levels of C."

Practically: draw the A×B interaction plot separately for each level of C. If those plots look the same, there is no three-way interaction. If the pattern of crossing changes from one panel to the next, there is.

Type I versus Type III sums of squares

Trap — R's anova() is order-dependent

anova() gives Type I (sequential) sums of squares: each term is tested in the order you typed it, adjusted only for terms before it.

On unbalanced data, changing the order of your formula changes the p-values. That is clearly unacceptable.

Type III (marginal) tests each term as if it were added last, so order stops mattering. In R: drop1(model, test="F"), and it requires options(contrasts = c("contr.sum", …)) first or the results are nonsense.

The saving grace

On balanced data, Type I and Type III give identical answers. The design is orthogonal, so there is nothing for the ordering to change.

Most exam data is balanced, so in practice you can read anova() output directly — but you need to know why that is safe, and that it stops being safe the moment the design is unbalanced.

Model selection

The principle of marginality, restated

Prune from the top down

If you keep a higher-order interaction, you must keep every lower-order term inside it. Keep A×B×C and you cannot delete A×B, even with a p-value of 0.99.

So model reduction always starts with the highest-order term and works downwards. You may never remove a main effect while an interaction containing it remains.

The partial F-test

How do you decide objectively whether a whole block of terms can go? You compare the full model with the reduced one.

F₀ = (SSE,reduced − SSE,full) / (dfE,reduced − dfE,full)SSE,full / dfE,full Top: the extra error you took on by simplifying, per degree of freedom you gave back.
Bottom: the error mean square of the big safe model. If the deleted terms were really zero, top ≈ bottom and F ≈ 1.
On the exam — worked, from the barley data

Full model SSE = 10522 on 16 df. Reduced (Site dropped entirely) SSE = 17179 on 24 df.

F₀ = (17179 − 10522) / (24 − 16)10522 / 16 = 6657 / 8657.6 = 1.265

Compare with F0.05, 8, 16 = 2.59. Since 1.265 < 2.59, fail to reject — the Site terms are noise and can be dropped.

Degrees of freedom for the test: numerator = df you gave back (8), denominator = df of the full model (16). Getting these the wrong way round is the built-in trap.

AIC and BIC

AIC = −2·ln(L) + 2k       BIC = −2·ln(L) + ln(N)·k Fit, plus a penalty for how many parameters you used. Lower is better, for both.
L = likelihood (how well it fits) · k = number of parameters · N = sample size.
AIC vs BIC — the difference that gets asked

The penalty per parameter is 2 for AIC, ln(N) for BIC. Once N > 8, ln(N) > 2, so BIC penalises complexity more harshly and selects smaller models.

BIC is "consistent" — with enough data it finds the true model if it is among the candidates. AIC aims at best prediction and tends to keep a few extra terms.

In R, step() automates the search, dropping terms one at a time while respecting marginality, until AIC stops improving.

Where the two halves of the course meet. This chapter contains the single highest-value fact in the whole compendium — read the denominator section twice.

The model

yijt = μ + ti + bj + (tb)ij + εijt Structurally identical to the fixed two-factor model, but every effect is now a random variable:
ti ~ N(0, σ²t) · bj ~ N(0, σ²b) · (tb)ij ~ N(0, σ²tb) · ε ~ N(0, σ²), all independent.
Notation convention — worth knowing for the exam

The lecture, and the exam papers, use Greek letters for fixed effects (τ, β) and Latin letters for random effects (t, b). So (τβ)ij is a fixed interaction and (tb)ij is a random one.

An exam question may hand you a list of symbols and ask which model equation is right — the letter case tells you which effects are random.

V(yijt) = σ²t + σ²b + σ²tb + σ² Four variance components now, instead of two. Each random term adds its own.

Expected mean squares — the whole lecture in four lines

E(MSA) = σ² + nσ²tb + bnσ²t
E(MSB) = σ² + nσ²tb + anσ²b
E(MSAB) = σ² + nσ²tb
E(MSE) = σ² Look at the first line. E(MS_A) contains the interaction variance σ²tb. That one fact drives everything below.

Why the denominator changes — the key idea

The reasoning, step by step

An F-test works by finding two mean squares whose expected values are identical under H₀ and differ only by the thing you are testing. Then the ratio is 1 under H₀ and bigger otherwise.

You want to test H₀: σ²t = 0. Look for a denominator whose expectation matches the numerator with only bnσ²t removed:

E(MSA)E(MSAB) = σ² + nσ²tb + bnσ²tσ² + nσ²tb  →  equals 1 exactly when σ²t = 0  ✓

Whereas dividing by MSE would give (σ² + nσ²tb + bnσ²t) ⁄ σ², which is bigger than 1 even when σ²t is zero, as long as there is any interaction. You could not tell a real main effect from a strong interaction.

REML, and why it exists

Method of moments vs REML

Method of moments — the subtractions above. Simple, unbiased, works by hand. Can produce negative variances, which are impossible.

REML (restricted maximum likelihood) — the computer searches iteratively for the variance values that make the observed data most likely. Always non-negative. Slightly biased but consistent. Used by lme4 and by mixlm with REML = TRUE.

On balanced data with positive estimates, the two methods agree. REML matters for unbalanced designs and weak effects.

Three rules

Two passes. First pass: answer everything you can do in under two minutes, and mark a guess on everything else as you go. Second pass: return to the marked ones. You are then never at risk of running out of time with blanks.

Sanity-check every F. An F below 1 is never significant — you can answer immediately without a table. An F in the tens is almost always significant.

When two options differ only by a square root, or by a factor of n, that is a signal you are being tested on exactly that step. Slow down for ten seconds.
