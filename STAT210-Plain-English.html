Before anything elseThe symbol problem

Statistics is not hard because the ideas are hard. It is hard because the ideas are written in a compressed shorthand that nobody teaches you to read. A formula is a sentence with most of the words removed.

Here is a formula from your course:

ȳi· Said aloud: "y bar i dot". It means: the average measurement in group number i.

That is all it says. Four characters replaced eight words. Once you know that a bar over a letter means "the average of", and that a dot in a subscript means "added up across everything in that position", the shorthand stops being a wall.

So this document does three things every time a new symbol appears:

  1. Shows you how to say it out loud. You cannot think about something you cannot pronounce.
  2. Translates it into an ordinary sentence. Every formula gets a plain-English caption underneath.
  3. Tells you what it is for. Symbols exist to do a job. Knowing the job makes them memorable.

The little brown boxes in the margin are the pronunciation guides. On a narrow screen they appear between paragraphs instead.

The colours mean something

Your lecturer uses three colours. They are the most useful thing in the whole course, so I use them everywhere too:

BlueThe baseline. Where things sit before any treatment does anything. The overall average.
GreenThe signal. How much a treatment pushed things away from that baseline. This is what your experiment is trying to find.
RedThe noise. Random variation between individuals that has nothing to do with the treatment. This is what makes the signal hard to see.
The whole course in one sentence

You measured some things. Some of the variation you see is caused by your treatment (green), and some is just random differences between individuals (red). Statistics is the business of measuring both and asking whether green is big enough, compared to red, to be believed.

Everything else — every formula, every table, every test — is a detail of how you measure those two things and compare them.

ȳ "y bar" A bar over a letter always means the average of. So ȳ is "the average of the y measurements".
i "eye" A label meaning which group. If you have 3 diets, i is 1, 2 or 3.
· "dot" A dot in the subscript means averaged over everything in that slot. ȳi· = averaged over all animals in group i.

The exampleSix piglets

Everything below uses one small experiment, so the numbers stay familiar. It is deliberately tiny — six animals — so you can check every calculation by hand.

The question: does adding soya to a piglet's diet change how much weight it gains?

Three group averages: 82, 83, 84. They are different. The entire question of this course is whether that difference is real or an accident.

Look at the raw numbers again before reading on. Within the very first group, one piglet weighs 80 and the other 84 — a gap of 4 kg, on exactly the same diet. The gap between the group averages is only 1 kg. So the differences inside a group are bigger than the differences between the groups. That is the answer, and everything that follows is a way of saying it precisely.

A control group is not optional

NONS gets no soya. Without it you would know how much piglets on soya weigh, but not whether that is more, less, or the same as piglets that got none. A comparison needs something to compare to.

Lecture 2, the core ideaSplitting each measurement into three pieces

Take one piglet: the first NONS piglet, weighing 80 kg. Ask where that 80 came from. The answer, in the course's model, has three parts:

  1. Start at the overall average of all piglets: 83 kg. This is the baseline — roughly what any piglet weighs.
  2. Adjust for its diet: minus 1 kg. The NONS group averages 82, which is 1 below the overall 83. So being on NONS is worth −1.
  3. Adjust for the individual animal: minus 2 kg. This particular piglet weighs 80, but its group averages 82. It is 2 below its own group, for reasons nothing to do with diet — genetics, appetite, luck.

Add them: 83 − 1 − 2 = 80. Exactly the measured weight.

yit = μ + τi + εit Said aloud: "y i t equals mu plus tau i plus epsilon i t".
In words: any measurement = the overall average + the effect of its group + that individual's own randomness.

That is the model. When lecturers say "the model", this line is what they mean. It is not a prediction machine — it is a statement about where each number comes from.

In plain words

The green arrows are what you are trying to detect. The red arrows are the fog you are trying to see through. When the fog is thicker than the thing you are looking for, you cannot honestly claim to have seen it.

Why the green adjustments must add up to zero

The three diet effects are −1, 0 and +1. Add them: zero. This is not a coincidence — it is a rule built into the model, written Σ τi = 0.

Why this rule exists

Without it the model is ambiguous. Suppose I said the baseline is 83 with adjustments of −1, 0, +1. You could equally say the baseline is 100 with adjustments of −18, −17, −16. Both give exactly the same six predicted weights, so the data can never tell them apart.

Forcing the adjustments to add to zero picks one answer and makes it meaningful: the baseline becomes "the overall average", and each adjustment becomes "how far this group sits from that average". That is the interpretation you actually want.

μ "mew" (Greek letter mu) The overall average across everything. Here, 83 kg.
τ "tow" (Greek letter tau) The effect of a treatment — how far one group sits from the overall average. Here, −1, 0 and +1.
ε "ep-si-lon" The leftover randomness for one individual. You can never observe it directly; you only estimate it.
Σ "sigma" Means add up everything that follows. Σ τi = 0 reads "all the τ values, added together, come to zero".

Lecture 2, the arithmeticAdding up the arrows

You now have, for every piglet, a green arrow and a red arrow. To get one number for "total signal" and one for "total noise", you add them all up. But there is a catch, and it is the reason for all the squaring you see in the formulas.

Why everything gets squared

Some arrows point up (+2) and some point down (−2). If you simply added them, they would cancel out and every total would be zero — useless.

Squaring a number makes it positive (−2 squared is 4, just like +2 squared is 4). So squaring first, then adding, measures how far things are from the middle without caring which direction. That is all the squares are for. They are not deep; they are a trick to stop plus and minus cancelling.

Do it for all six piglets. Three columns: total distance from the overall average, the green part, and the red part.

And notice: 4 + 18 = 22. The green total and the red total add up exactly to the grand total. Nothing is lost, nothing is counted twice. This is the single most important fact in the course.

SST = SSTr + SSE Said aloud: "S S total equals S S treatment plus S S error".
In words: total variation = variation caused by the treatment + variation that is just noise. Here: 22 = 4 + 18.
Careful — the most common hand-calculation mistake

The green column adds to 4, not 2. There are only three green values (−1, 0, +1), but each one gets counted once for every piglet in that group. With 2 piglets per group, each counts twice.

This is why the written formula has an n in front of it — n being the number of animals per group. Forget the n and your answer is wrong by a factor of 2 (or 10, in a bigger experiment).

Reading the result

Of the 22 units of total variation, 4 come from diet and 18 come from individual differences. Diet accounts for 4 out of 22 — about 18%. The other 82% is noise. That is a weak signal, and the formal test is about to confirm it.

SS "S S", or "sum of squares" A total of squared distances. Just a number measuring "how much variation is here".
SSE "S S error" The noise total. "Error" here does not mean a mistake — it means variation the model cannot explain.
n "en" How many measurements in each group. Here n = 2.
N "big en" How many measurements in total. Here N = 6. Capital and small letters mean different things — this catches people out.
a "ay" How many groups. Here a = 3.

Lecture 2, the verdictDegrees of freedom, and the F number

You cannot compare 4 against 18 directly, and the reason is worth getting right.

Why you cannot just compare 4 and 18

The noise total (18) was built by adding up six numbers. The signal total (4) was built from only three group averages. The noise total has more numbers feeding into it, so it would win just by having more terms — regardless of whether there is any real noise.

It is like comparing the total weight of a shopping bag with 20 items to one with 3 items. To compare fairly you need the average weight per item, not the total. That is exactly what comes next.

So you divide each total by how many independent pieces of information went into it. That count is called the degrees of freedom.

What "degrees of freedom" actually means

It is the number of values that were genuinely free to be anything, once the constraints are accounted for.

Take the three diet effects: −1, 0, +1. They are forced to add to zero. So if I tell you the first two are −1 and 0, the third has to be +1 — it has no freedom left. Three numbers, but only two of them were free. Degrees of freedom for the treatment = 2.

Same logic for noise: six measurements, but you used the data to work out three group averages, and each average you calculate uses up one piece of freedom. 6 − 3 = 3 degrees of freedom for the noise.

These per-degree-of-freedom averages are called mean squares. Now they are comparable, so divide one by the other:

F = 26 = 0.33 Said aloud: "F equals nought point three three".
In words: the signal is one third the size of the noise.
How to read any F number

F is signal divided by noise. That is the whole thing.

  • F near 1 — signal is about the same size as noise. Nothing to see. This is what you expect when the treatment does nothing.
  • F much bigger than 1 — signal towers over noise. Something real is probably happening.
  • F less than 1 — the signal is smaller than the noise. Definitely nothing to see.

Our F is 0.33 — less than 1. The differences between diets are smaller than the differences between piglets on the same diet.

Is 0.33 small enough to be "nothing"? The p-value

Even when a treatment does absolutely nothing, F will not come out at exactly 1 — random luck makes it wobble. So you need to know: if the diets truly had no effect, how often would luck alone produce an F of 0.33 or bigger?

The answer here is 74% of the time. Written as a decimal, p = 0.74.

What a p-value is, plainly

A p-value answers exactly one question: "if there were really no effect at all, how likely is it that I would see results at least as extreme as mine, purely by chance?"

p = 0.74 means: three-quarters of the time, pure luck produces results like this. So this result is completely unremarkable.

The usual cut-off is 0.05 — 5%. Below that, results this extreme would be rare enough by luck alone that people are willing to believe something real is happening. It is a convention, not a law of nature.

Careful — the sentence that loses marks

Do not write "soya has no effect on weight gain."

Write: "There is no statistically significant evidence that diet affects weight gain (F = 0.33, p = 0.74). With only 2 piglets per group this study has very low power, so a real effect could easily have gone undetected."

The difference matters. Failing to find something is not the same as showing it is not there. A study this small could miss an elephant.

df "D F", or "degrees of freedom" How many values were genuinely free to vary. Used to turn totals into fair averages.
MS "M S", or "mean square" A sum of squares divided by its degrees of freedom. An average amount of variation.
F "F" Signal divided by noise. Named after the statistician Ronald Fisher.
p "P", or "the p-value" The chance of seeing a result this extreme if nothing real were going on. Small p = surprising = interesting.
α "alpha" The cut-off you decide on in advance, almost always 0.05. If p is below α, you call the result significant.

Lecture 2, the fine printWhat has to be true for any of this to work

The p-value of 0.74 was calculated assuming three things about your data. If they are false, the number is wrong and you would not know it.

The assumptionIn plain wordsHow you check
Independence Each piglet's weight does not depend on any other piglet's. They are not littermates sharing a pen and competing for food. You cannot test this afterwards. You buy it at the start by assigning animals to diets at random. That is what randomisation is for.
Normality The red arrows — the individual randomness — follow the familiar bell-shaped pattern. Most small, a few large, symmetric. A picture called a QQ plot: if the points sit roughly on a straight line, you are fine. Or a formal test called Shapiro–Wilk.
Equal spread All three diets produce roughly the same amount of individual scatter. One group is not far more variable than another. A picture called residuals vs fitted: you do not want a funnel or cone shape. Or a test called Bartlett's.
Careful — two things people get backwards

1. Normality applies to the red arrows, not the raw weights. If the diets really work, the weights will cluster into several separate humps — which is not bell-shaped, and is not a problem. Always check the leftovers, not the original data.

2. For these checks, a large p-value is the good outcome. Everywhere else in the course a small p is the exciting one. Here the thing being tested is "the assumption holds", so you want not to reject it.

Both piglet checks pass — Shapiro–Wilk gives p = 0.11, Bartlett gives p = 0.83. But with six animals these tests are nearly blind; passing them is weak reassurance, not proof.

Why more measurements help — the one bit of algebra worth knowing

Individual piglets vary. You cannot change that. But averages of several piglets vary much less than individual piglets do.

Specifically, if individual measurements have a spread of σ², then the average of n of them has a spread of σ²/n. Average 10 animals and the wobble in your estimate is a tenth of what it was.

So bigger groups give you sharper group averages, and sharper averages let you see smaller real differences through the noise. This one fact is behind every "collect more data" recommendation in statistics.

The word for this is power — the chance that your study will detect a real effect if one exists. Power goes up when: you measure more individuals, the true effect is larger, or the individuals are less variable to begin with.

σ "sigma" (small letter) The spread of individual measurements. Confusingly, capital Σ means "add up" and small σ means "spread" — they are unrelated.
σ² "sigma squared" Called the variance. Spread, measured in squared units. σ on its own is the standard deviation — the same thing back in normal units.
MSE "M S error" Your best estimate of σ² from the data. For the piglets it is 6. This number reappears in every calculation from here on.

Lecture 3How sure are we? Confidence, and two kinds of mistake

Lecture 3 looks like a pile of different tests. It is one test, reused. Here it is:

T = what you measured − what you are testing againsthow much your measurement wobbles In words: how big is the difference, compared to how uncertain we are about it?
In plain words

Suppose you find a 1 kg difference. Is that impressive? It depends entirely on your precision. If your measurements are accurate to within 0.1 kg, a 1 kg difference is enormous. If they wobble by 5 kg, a 1 kg difference is nothing.

T is that comparison as a single number. The top is the effect; the bottom is the wobble. A big T means the effect is large relative to the uncertainty.

The wobble on the bottom is called the standard error. Every quantity you might want to test has its own formula for it, but they all use the same noise estimate MSE = 6 that you already calculated:

What you want to say something aboutIts standard error
One group's average√MSE ÷ n — noise, divided by group size
The overall average√MSE ÷ N — noise, divided by total size
The gap between two groups√MSE × (1/n₁ + 1/n₂)

Worked through: is the control group's average really 83?

  1. What you measured: the NONS group averages 82.
  2. What you are testing against: the value 83.
  3. The wobble: √(6 ÷ 2) = √3 = 1.73.
  4. Put it together: T = (82 − 83) ÷ 1.73 = −0.58.
  5. Compare to the threshold. With 3 degrees of freedom the cut-off is 3.18. Since 0.58 is much smaller, this is not a significant difference.
  6. The p-value is 0.60 — you would see a gap this big 60% of the time by luck alone.

Confidence intervals — usually more useful than the test

Instead of a yes/no answer, give a range of plausible values:

what you measured  ±  threshold × wobble For NONS: 82 ± 3.18 × 1.73 = 82 ± 5.5, giving the range 76.5 to 87.5 kg.

That range is 11 kg wide, on animals weighing about 83 kg. The three diets' ranges overlap almost entirely. The honest summary of this experiment is that it was too small to learn anything. A confidence interval says that far more clearly than a p-value does.

A shortcut worth remembering

A confidence interval and a test are the same thing in two outfits. If the value you are testing falls outside the interval, the test rejects it. If it falls inside, the test does not.

83 is inside 76.5–87.5, so we cannot reject it. That is the same conclusion we got the long way round in step 6 above. Once you have an interval, you never need to run the test.

Careful — what "95% confident" does not mean

It does not mean "there is a 95% chance the true value is in this range". The true average weight is a fixed number; it is either in the range or it is not.

What it means is: if you repeated this whole experiment many times, 95% of the ranges you built this way would contain the true value. The 95% describes the reliability of the method, not this particular range.

Lecture 4Which groups actually differ?

The F test answers one question: is anything at all going on? It does not say which groups differ. Your lecturer's phrase is worth keeping: the F test tells you there is a fire, but not which room is burning.

The piglet study never rejected, so it stops here. For lecture 4 the course switches to a bigger experiment where the F test did reject.

A contrast is a specific question

A contrast is just a comparison you write down using weights. You give each group a number, and those numbers must add up to zero.

The question you want to askS00S05S10S15Add up to
Is the control different from the 5 g group?+1−1000 ✓
Do the two higher doses differ from each other?00+1−10 ✓
Do low doses differ from high doses?+1+1−1−10 ✓
Is the control different from all the treated fish?+1−⅓−⅓−⅓0 ✓
Why the weights must add to zero

Because you want to measure a difference, not a level.

Imagine every fish suddenly had 100 more units of haemoglobin. Every group average goes up by 100. A genuine comparison should be completely unaffected — the gaps between groups have not changed.

When the weights add to zero, the +100 gets multiplied by weights that cancel out, and the answer is unchanged. If they did not add to zero, your "comparison" would shift whenever the overall level shifted, which would make it useless. Adding to zero is what makes it a comparison rather than a measurement.

Working one out

  1. Multiply and add. Using +1 and −1 on the first two groups: (+1 × 7.20) + (−1 × 9.33) = −2.13. The control has 2.13 units less haemoglobin.
  2. Work out the wobble. This comes to 0.56.
  3. Divide. −2.13 ÷ 0.56 = −3.80. The gap is nearly four times the size of the uncertainty.
  4. Compare to the threshold, which is 2.03 here. 3.80 is bigger, so this is a real difference.

Lecture 4, the dangerWhy testing everything is cheating

With 4 groups there are 6 possible pairs to compare. Why not just test all 6?

Because false alarms pile up

Each individual test has a 5% chance of a false alarm. That sounds small. But run 6 tests and the chance that at least one of them raises a false alarm is 26.5%.

Think of it like rolling dice. One roll rarely gives a six. Roll six times and a six becomes likely. Each test is a roll, and a false alarm is the six.

GroupsPairs to testChance of at least one false alarmWhat that means
3314%Already three times worse than the 5% you intended
4627%The trout study — one in four chance of a false claim
51040%Close to a coin flip
104590%You will almost certainly claim something false

This has a name — the family-wise error rate — and it is a documented major cause of published research that later turns out to be wrong.

The rule that follows from this

Run the overall F test first. Only if it rejects may you look at individual comparisons. And when you do, use a correction.

Skipping straight to comparing pairs has a name — fishing — and it is treated as misconduct, not as a shortcut.

The three corrections

All three work the same way: they raise the bar, so you need stronger evidence before calling something significant. They differ in what they are protecting you against.

NameWhat it doesUse it when
Bonferroni Divide your 5% by the number of tests. With 6 tests, each must beat 0.83% instead of 5%. You have a few specific comparisons, decided before you saw the data. Simple, but gets harsh fast.
Tukey Works out exactly how big the largest gap between any two groups tends to get by luck alone, and uses that as the bar. You want to compare all pairs. This is the usual choice, and the most powerful one for that job.
Scheffé Protects against every conceivable comparison, including ones you thought of only after looking at the data. You went looking for patterns first. Safest, but hardest to pass.
Why Tukey is better than Bonferroni for comparing all pairs

Bonferroni treats the 6 comparisons as 6 unrelated problems and charges you full price for each.

Tukey knows something Bonferroni ignores: if the biggest gap between any two groups is not significant, then no smaller gap can be either. So it only needs to control that single biggest gap, rather than guarding all 6 separately. Solving the real problem instead of the worst imaginable one is what lets it keep more of your ability to detect real effects.

ReferenceWhat to do in an exam

  1. Write down a, n and N first. Number of groups, number per group, total. Every degrees-of-freedom calculation depends on them, and getting them down stops most errors.
  2. Work out the group averages and the overall average. Everything else is built from these.
  3. Fill in the table. Sums of squares, then degrees of freedom, then divide to get mean squares, then divide again for F. Check that your signal and noise totals add to the grand total — if they do not, you have made an arithmetic slip.
  4. Compare F to the threshold from the printed table, using degrees of freedom (groups − 1) across the top and (total − groups) down the side.
  5. State the conclusion in a full sentence, including the F value, the degrees of freedom, and the p-value. If you did not reject, mention power.
  6. Only if you rejected, go on to comparisons — and say which correction you used and why.
Careful — round only at the very end

Keep all the decimal places through the middle of a calculation and round only your final answer. Rounding halfway can change a borderline result. Two of the numbers printed in your own lecture slides are slightly wrong for exactly this reason.

If you remember nothing else

Split the variation into the part your treatment explains and the part it does not. Divide each by how many independent pieces of information built it. Compare the two. If the first is much bigger than the second, believe the effect.

That is the entire course. Everything else is detail about how to do those four steps carefully.
