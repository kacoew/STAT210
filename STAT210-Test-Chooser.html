The two df people get wrong

Two random factors: the denominator df is (a−1)(b−1) — the interaction's df — not ab(n−1). If you change the denominator mean square, you must change the denominator degrees of freedom to match it.

Additive model: once the interaction is pooled into the error, the error df becomes N − a − b + 1, not ab(n−1). That is the whole point of dropping it — you gain (a−1)(b−1) degrees of freedom, which is where the extra power comes from.

Chart 3 · family BWhich levels differ? Contrasts and post-hoc

The F-test tells you there is a fire but not which room is burning. What you are allowed to do next depends entirely on when you decided what to compare.

Reading a TukeyHSD table — the only skill actually tested

R prints one row per pair with a difference, a lower and upper bound, and an adjusted p-value. If the interval contains zero, that pair is not significantly different. That is the whole reading.

These are simultaneous intervals — all pairs together have 95% coverage — so they are wider than six separate 95% intervals would be. That is by design, not an error.

Trap — the two χ² labelling conventions are exact opposites

Your printed table booklet labels its columns by upper-tail area α. R's qchisq() labels by cumulative probability. For 3 degrees of freedom:

Source"0.025""0.975"
Exam table booklet (upper-tail area)9.35 big0.22 small
qchisq() in R (cumulative)0.22 small9.35 big

Lecture 3 writes the interval one way and lecture 5 writes it the other. Both are correct in their own convention, which is exactly why writing the formula symbolically is dangerous.

The safe rule, which works under either convention: you will be handed two chi-square numbers. Divide SSE by the larger one to get the lower bound, and by the smaller one to get the upper bound. Never reason from the subscript.

The 2025 paper gave χ²0.975,21 = 10.28 and χ²0.025,21 = 35.48 — the table convention. Dividing 55.727 by 35.48 and 10.28 gives [1.57, 5.42]. Getting them the wrong way round gives an "interval" running from 5.42 down to 1.57, which is one of the offered distractors.

There is no exact interval for σ²τ

The estimator is a difference of two mean squares, which follows no standard distribution. The course uses Satterthwaite's approximation to get an effective degrees of freedom, and the lecture states explicitly that you are not required to compute this by hand. Knowing why it is hard is the examinable part.

Two reversals to remember

1. For assumption tests (Shapiro–Wilk, Bartlett) a large p-value is the good outcome — H₀ there is "the assumption holds". Everywhere else in the course a small p is the interesting one.

2. Normality is assumed of the residuals, not the raw data. If the treatments work, the raw data is a mixture of several means and will look multi-humped. That is expected, not a violation.

What a log transform does to the interpretation

Fitting ln(y) = μ + τi + ε is the same as saying the original data are multiplicative: y = eμ·eτi·eε. So on the original scale:

  • Back-transforming a mean gives a geometric mean, not an arithmetic one
  • A difference on the log scale is a ratio on the original scale — a log difference of 1.269 means one group is e1.269 = 3.56 times the other, not 1.269 units more
  • A CI [L, U] on the log scale becomes [eL, eU] — an interval for the ratio

Three rules that govern all simplification

Marginality. Keep an interaction → you must keep every lower-order term inside it, whatever their p-values. Always prune from the top down.

BIC is stricter than AIC. The penalty per parameter is 2 for AIC and ln(N) for BIC. Once N > 8, ln(N) > 2, so BIC selects smaller models.

Type I = Type III on balanced data. They differ only when the design is unbalanced, where anova() becomes order-dependent and you need drop1() instead.

The 30-second routine, for when you freeze
  1. Write down a, b, n, N.
  2. Mark every factor FIXED or RANDOM.
  3. Which of the five families is this question in? → Chart 1
  4. Find the row in the master table. Copy the df straight off it.
  5. Compute. Then sanity-check: F below 1 is never significant.
  6. Asked for σ or σ²? Check which before answering.
