Two Kinds of Variation

Until now, your groups were things you picked on purpose. This lecture asks what changes when the groups are just a random sample from a much bigger population — and the answer is: the question you are asking changes completely.

Third in the plain-language set Every number checked against the real sires data

Start hereWhat is actually new

Your lecturer calls this "one of the biggest conceptual leaps in applied statistics" and tells you to be patient with yourself. He is right. The maths barely changes. The meaning changes entirely, and that is much harder.

Here is the whole lecture in one comparison. In the trout experiment from lecture 4, you chose four doses of a drug: 0 g, 5 g, 10 g, 15 g. You chose them deliberately. If you ran the study again, you would use those exact same four doses.

Now a different study. You want to know how much a bull's genetics affect his daughters' milk yield. There is a national breeding register with thousands of bulls. You cannot test them all, so you pick 5 at random and measure 8 daughters of each.

The leap

You do not care about those 5 particular bulls. They are not interesting. They are just a sample.

What you want to know is about bulls in general: how much do bulls vary? If you picked a different 5 tomorrow, you would want the same answer.

That difference — chosen on purpose versus drawn at random — is what separates fixed effects (lectures 2 to 4) from random effects (this lecture).

The modelOne symbol changes its nature

The written model is identical to the one you already know:

yit = μ + τi + εit Any measurement = the overall average + the effect of its group + individual randomness.

Same letters, same arrangement. But one of them has quietly changed from a fixed unknown number into a random variable.

SymbolIn lectures 2–4 it wasNow it is
μA fixed unknown number — the overall averageUnchanged. Still a fixed number, now meaning the average of the whole population.
τiA fixed unknown number — this group's effectNow a random variable, drawn from a bell curve with average 0 and spread σ²τ
εitRandom individual noiseUnchanged.
In plain words

Before, "the effect of diet S1" was a specific number sitting out there in the world, waiting to be estimated. It had one true value.

Now, "the effect of a bull" is not one number — every bull in the register has his own, and they are scattered around zero. When you draw a bull at random, you draw his effect at random too. So τ is no longer a fixed thing you estimate; it is a lottery you sample from.

And the thing you want to know is not which ticket you drew. It is how spread out the tickets are.

Written formally, the new assumption is:

τi ~ N(0, σ²τ) Said aloud: "tau i is normally distributed with mean nought and variance sigma squared tau".
In words: group effects follow a bell curve centred on zero, with a spread we call σ²τ.

Two consequences fall straight out of this

First — every group now has the same expected value

E(yit) = μ + E(τi) + E(εit) = μ + 0 + 0 = μ Because τ averages to zero across the population, every group has the same long-run average, μ.

In the fixed model, group 1 was expected to sit at μ + τ₁ and group 2 at μ + τ₂ — different places. Here, before you know which bull you drew, every group is expected to sit at μ. The differences you actually see are the luck of the draw.

Second — and this is the important one — variance now has two sources

V(yit) = σ²τ + σ² Said aloud: "the variance of y equals sigma squared tau plus sigma squared".
In words: how much a cow's yield varies = how much bulls vary + how much cows of the same bull vary.
Why there are suddenly two

In the fixed model, τi was a constant, and constants have no variance — they never wobble. So the only thing that varied was the individual noise, and V(y) = σ².

Now τi is a random variable, so it wobbles too, and it contributes its own variance. Pick a cow at random from the country and two independent things happened to her: her father was drawn from the bull population (contributing σ²τ) and she turned out however she turned out (contributing σ²). Both add to how unpredictable she is.

This is why the whole framework is also called the variance components model — you are decomposing the total variance into components.

What you are trying to estimate now

Not group averages. The two variances.

  • σ²τ — between-sire variance. How much genuine genetic quality differs from bull to bull across the whole register.
  • σ² — within-sire variance. How much daughters of the same bull differ, because of feed, environment, and chance.
σ²τ "sigma squared tau" The between-group variance — how much the groups themselves vary in the population. This is the new symbol of the lecture.
σ² "sigma squared" The within-group variance — how much individuals in the same group differ. Same meaning as in lectures 2–4.
E( ) "the expected value of" The long-run average of something if you repeated it forever.
V( ) "the variance of" How spread out something is. Also written Var( ).
~ "is distributed as" Says which pattern a random quantity follows. N means the bell curve (normal).

A side effectSisters are alike, and that breaks an old assumption

Here is something that did not happen in the fixed model. Take two daughters of the same bull. Are their yields related?

Yes — because they share the same τ. Whatever genetic luck their father had, both of them got it. If he happened to be an above-average bull, both daughters are pushed up together.

Cov(yit, yik) = σ²τ Said aloud: "the covariance of y i t and y i k equals sigma squared tau".
In words: two individuals in the same group are related to each other, by exactly the amount that groups vary.
Careful — this breaks the independence assumption

In lectures 2 to 4, one of the three assumptions was that all observations are independent. In a random effects model that is deliberately false: observations inside a group are positively correlated, on purpose, because they share a random effect.

This is exactly why ordinary regression tools are not enough here, and why the lecture introduces special software. If you feed correlated data to a method that assumes independence, it thinks it has more information than it really does, and your confidence intervals come out too narrow.

The ICC — turning that correlation into a number you can report

The correlation between two members of the same group has a name: the intraclass correlation coefficient, or ICC. It is just the between-group variance as a fraction of the total.

ICC = σ²τσ²τ + σ² In words: of all the variation in milk yield, what fraction is due to which bull the cow's father was?

Because both variances are positive, this always lands between 0 and 1.

ICCWhat it means
0Group membership tells you nothing at all. Knowing the father is useless for predicting the daughter.
0.1818% of the variation is down to the group. Knowing the father helps a bit.
1All variation is between groups. Knowing the father tells you the answer exactly.
The intuition — your lecturer's classroom analogy

Picture a school with many classrooms, each with a different teacher.

If teachers vary a lot in quality, but students within any one classroom all score similarly, then knowing which classroom a child is in tells you a lot about their grade — high ICC.

If all teachers are much of a muchness, but students within each classroom are wildly different from each other, then knowing the classroom tells you almost nothing — low ICC.

So: ICC answers "how much does knowing the group help me predict the individual?"

Cov "covariance" How much two things move together. Zero means unrelated; positive means they rise and fall together.
ICC "I C C" Intraclass correlation coefficient. The fraction of total variation that comes from the groups. Between 0 and 1.

Why the same F works for a completely different question

This is the mathematical heart of the lecture. It rests on two facts about what the mean squares are expected to equal in the long run.

E(MSE) = σ²
E(MSTr) = σ² + n·σ²τ Said aloud: "the expected mean square for error is sigma squared; the expected mean square for treatment is sigma squared plus n sigma squared tau".
Why this makes the F-test work

Read the second line carefully. MSTr is expected to equal the noise plus an extra bit, and that extra bit is n × σ²τ — the between-group variance, multiplied by group size.

If bulls really do not vary (σ²τ = 0), the extra bit disappears and both mean squares are estimating the same thing, σ². Their ratio should sit near 1.

If bulls do vary (σ²τ > 0), the top of the fraction is inflated and F gets pushed up.

So a large F is evidence of between-group variance — which is precisely the new question. Same statistic, and it happens to answer both questions because both nulls make the two mean squares equal.

Notice the n

The extra bit is n × σ²τ, not just σ²τ. More daughters per bull multiplies your ability to see between-bull variance, because each group average becomes a sharper measurement of its own bull. This is the same "averages are more precise than individuals" idea from lecture 2, showing up again.

The verdict for the sires

F = 2.806, and the table value for 4 and 35 degrees of freedom at 5% is 2.641. Since 2.806 is bigger, reject the null: there is evidence that bulls genuinely vary in genetic quality. The p-value is 0.0404.

Careful — this only just passes

p = 0.0404 is below 0.05, but not by much. F = 2.806 against a threshold of 2.641 clears the bar by about 6%.

Remember this when you reach the confidence intervals later on — it explains why they turn out so uninformative. A barely-significant test and a nearly-useless confidence interval are two views of the same weak evidence.

E(MS) "expected mean square" What a mean square equals on average in the long run. Abbreviated EMS. Your lecturer says this is the backbone of everything from here to the end of the course.
H₀ "H nought" The null hypothesis — the boring possibility you are trying to rule out.

Getting the numbersMethod of moments

You now know bulls vary. By how much? The trick is simple and rather elegant: take the two expected-mean-square equations, pretend the observed values equal the expected values, and solve.

  1. The error one is immediate. Since E(MSE) = σ², your estimate is just σ̂² = MSE. For the sires: 111,462.
  2. Take the treatment equation and substitute what you just found: MSTr = MSE + n·σ̂²τ.
  3. Rearrange for the unknown. Subtract, then divide by n.
σ̂²τ = MSTr − MSEn In words: take how much bigger the treatment mean square is than the error mean square, and divide by the group size.

For the sires

σ̂²τ = 312,752 − 111,4628 = 201,2908 = 25,161 Between-bull variance: 25,161. Within-bull variance: 111,462.

The ICC follows in one line

ICC = 25,16125,161 + 111,462 = 25,161136,623 = 0.184 18.4% of all the variation in milk yield is down to which bull the cow's father was. The remaining 81.6% is environment, feed, and individual chance.

The flaw in this method

Careful — you can get a negative variance

Look at the formula again: (MSTr − MSE) ÷ n. Nothing stops MSE from being bigger than MSTr. When that happens the formula returns a negative variance — which is impossible, since variance is an average of squared things.

This is not a mistake in your arithmetic. It happens by pure bad luck when the true between-group variance is small and your sample is small.

What to do: report the estimate as zero, or switch to a method called REML, which cannot produce negative values.

The sires escaped this — 312,752 is comfortably bigger than 111,462 — but only because F came out above 1. Any dataset with F below 1 gives a negative estimate.

σ̂²τ "sigma squared tau hat" The hat means "our estimate from data", as opposed to the unknown true value.
REML "rem-ell" Restricted Maximum Likelihood. A different way of estimating variances that never returns a negative number. The computer finds the answer by trial and improvement rather than a formula.

Confidence intervalsThree of them, and they behave very differently

1 — For the population average μ, and a textbook error

In the random model, μ means the true average milk yield of the entire national herd, not just your 40 cows. The best estimate is still the grand mean, 6518 litres. But the uncertainty around it is not what it was in the fixed model.

Why the uncertainty is bigger than you would expect

In the fixed model you had 40 independent measurements, so the wobble in the grand mean was σ²/40.

But your 40 cows are not 40 independent pieces of information about the national herd. They come from only 5 bulls. If those 5 bulls happened to be above average, all 40 cows are above average together, and no amount of measuring more daughters would reveal that.

Your effective sample size for learning about the nation is closer to 5 than to 40. Adding daughters sharpens each bull's average, but it does not add new bulls.

V(μ̂) = σ² + n·σ²τa·n   which we estimate as   MSTra·n The top is exactly what MSTr estimates, so the whole thing collapses to MSTr divided by the total number of observations.
Careful — your textbook is wrong here, and your lecturer says so

The degrees of freedom for this interval are a − 1 = 4, not N − a = 35.

Montgomery, 10th edition, page 107, states N − a. That is an error. Your lecturer flags it explicitly on the slides, so it is fair game for an exam question.

The reasoning: you built this standard error out of MSTr, so you inherit that mean square's degrees of freedom — which is a − 1. You never used MSE, so its 35 degrees of freedom are irrelevant here.

The calculation, step by step

  1. The estimate: ȳ·· = 6518.15 litres.
  2. The standard error: √312,752 ÷ 40 = √7818.8 = 88.42.
  3. The multiplier: with 4 degrees of freedom, t = 2.776.
  4. Put it together: 6518.15 ± 2.776 × 88.42 = 6518.15 ± 245.5.
  5. The interval: 6272.6 to 6763.7 litres.

2 — For the within-group variance σ², which is well behaved

Nothing has changed from lecture 3. The same exact interval applies:

[ SSEχ²0.975, 35 , SSEχ²0.025, 35 ] = [ 3,901,16653.20 , 3,901,16620.57 ] Giving 73,326 to 189,659. Remember: the big chi-square number gives the small end of the interval.

This one is exact and reliable, because it rests on 35 degrees of freedom — you have plenty of information about how much daughters of the same bull differ.

3 — For the between-group variance σ²τ, which is a mess

Why this one is hard

Your estimate is (MSTr − MSE) ÷ n — a difference of two different mean squares. Each has its own known distribution, but their difference does not follow any standard, named distribution. There is no exact formula.

The workaround is Satterthwaite's approximation: pretend the difference behaves like a chi-square distribution, and work out what degrees of freedom would make that pretence fit best. The answer comes out as a fraction rather than a whole number.

For the sires, that effective degrees of freedom works out to ν = 1.63.

Read that number again

1.63 effective degrees of freedom. You measured 40 cows, and you have less than two degrees of freedom' worth of information about how much bulls vary.

That is the honest cost of sampling only 5 bulls. If you want to learn about bull-to-bull variation, you need more bulls — not more daughters.

Your lecturer says you are not required to compute this by hand in STAT210. Understanding why it is hard is the examinable part.

And a confidence interval for the ICC — with a surprise

There is a formula for this, using two F-table values:

L = 1n( F₀F0.025 − 1 )     U = 1n( F₀F0.975 − 1 ) then the interval for the ICC is [ L ÷ (1+L) , U ÷ (1+U) ].

Run it on the sires and you get −0.015 to 0.739.

The lower end is negative — and an ICC cannot be negative

Your lecture slide prints this result without comment. It is not a mistake in the formula; it is the interval-shaped version of the negative variance problem from earlier. In practice you truncate it: report the interval as 0 to 0.74.

Why it happens — and why it does not contradict the F-test

This looks like a contradiction. The F-test rejected the null that σ²τ = 0, yet the confidence interval for the ICC includes zero. How can both be true?

Because they are not the same test. Look at which thresholds each one uses:

  • The F-test is one-sided — a variance can only be too big, never too small. It compares F₀ = 2.806 against 2.641, and 2.806 clears it.
  • The confidence interval is two-sided, splitting the 5% into 2.5% at each end. That effectively requires F₀ to beat 3.179, and 2.806 does not.

So F₀ = 2.806 sits between the two thresholds: enough for the one-sided test, not enough for the two-sided interval. Both results are correct, and together they tell you the truth better than either alone: there is probably some genetic variation, but you cannot pin down how much.

ν "new" (Greek letter nu) Effective degrees of freedom from Satterthwaite's approximation. Usually a fraction, not a whole number.
χ² "kai squared" The distribution used for questions about variances. Lopsided, never negative.
F₀ "F nought" The F value you calculated from your data, as opposed to the threshold from a table.

On the computerWhy base R is not enough

The problem with the ordinary R commands

The functions you have used so far — aov() and lm() — compute the mean squares perfectly well. But they have no idea which of your factors are random. They always divide by MSE when forming an F-test.

For this one-factor design that happens to give the right answer. For the multi-factor designs coming in lecture 6, it gives the wrong F-test, silently. You need software that understands the expected-mean-square structure.

The course uses a package called mixlm. The one rule is: wrap any random factor in r().

# sire is RANDOM — note the r( ) around it
model <- lm(milk ~ r(sire), data = sires)

# without r( ), sire would be treated as FIXED
model <- lm(milk ~ sire, data = sires)
Something to know about your own computer

I checked. mixlm and lme4 are not currently installed on your machine — so the R code on the lecture slides will not run as-is. To install them, open RStudio and type this into the console at the bottom left, then press enter:

install.packages(c("mixlm", "lme4"))

It will download from the internet and take a minute or two. You only ever need to do it once. After that, library(mixlm) at the top of a script makes the commands available.

Getting every number without any package at all

You do not actually need mixlm to answer the exam questions on this lecture — because, as we saw, the ANOVA table is identical to the fixed-effects one. Everything else is arithmetic you can do yourself. This is exactly how I checked every figure in this document:

# --- the ordinary ANOVA gives you the raw material -------------
m  <- aov(milk ~ sire, data = sires)
at <- anova(m)

MSTr <- at["sire",      "Mean Sq"]   # 312752
MSE  <- at["Residuals", "Mean Sq"]   # 111462
SSE  <- at["Residuals", "Sum Sq"]    # 3901166
a <- 5; n <- 8; N <- 40

# --- the two variance components -------------------------------
sigma2     <- MSE                     # 111462
sigma2_tau <- (MSTr - MSE) / n        # 25161

# --- the ICC ---------------------------------------------------
sigma2_tau / (sigma2_tau + sigma2)    # 0.1842

# --- confidence interval for mu, with df = a-1 -----------------
ybar  <- mean(sires$milk)
se_mu <- sqrt(MSTr / (a * n))
ybar + c(-1, 1) * qt(0.975, a - 1) * se_mu    # 6272.6  6763.7

# --- confidence interval for sigma^2 ---------------------------
SSE / qchisq(c(0.975, 0.025), N - a)          # 73326  189659

Every number in this document came out of those few lines.

The last ideaBLUPs, and why they shrink

One loose end. You said you did not care about the 5 specific bulls — but in real breeding work, of course you do. You want to know which bull to buy. Can you rank them?

Yes, but not by their raw daughter averages. The proper tool is a BLUP.

Why the raw average is not the best guess

Aage's 8 daughters averaged 6689 litres — the highest of the five. But how much of that is Aage being genuinely good, and how much is that he happened to get 8 lucky daughters?

With only 8 measurements, a fair chunk is luck. So the sensible estimate is not "6689". It is something pulled back toward the overall average, because extreme observed results are usually partly flukes that will not repeat.

This pulling-back is called shrinkage, and how far you pull depends on how much you trust the group: lots of daughters and big genuine bull-to-bull differences → trust the average, shrink little. Few daughters and small real differences → shrink a lot.

For the sires, the shrinkage factor works out at 0.64 — each bull's average gets pulled 36% of the way back to the overall mean of 6518.

Exam checklist for a random-effects question

  1. Say why the factor is random. "The 5 sires were drawn at random from a large register, so we want to generalise to the whole population, not to these 5." Examiners want to see you understand why, not just that you spotted the word.
  2. Build the ANOVA table exactly as in lecture 2. Nothing new.
  3. State the hypotheses in the new form: H₀: σ²τ = 0 against HA: σ²τ > 0, and say it is one-sided because a variance cannot be negative.
  4. Estimate both variance components using method of moments, and check the between-group one is not negative.
  5. Compute the ICC and say what it means in the context of the question — a percentage of variation, not just a number.
  6. If asked for a CI for μ, use df = a − 1. Say why: the standard error came from MSTr. Mentioning that the textbook gets this wrong will not hurt you.

If you remember nothing else from lecture 5

When groups are a random sample rather than a deliberate choice, you stop asking "which group is best?" and start asking "how much do groups vary?"

The arithmetic does not change. The ANOVA table is identical. What changes is that the treatment effect is now a random variable with its own variance, so total variation splits into two components — between-group and within-group — and the ICC is simply the first as a fraction of the total.
