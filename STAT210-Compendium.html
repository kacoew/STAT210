The load-bearing idea

ANOVA squares those arrows and adds them up. Sum the squared green arrows and you get the signal. Sum the squared red arrows and you get the noise. The F-test is their ratio. Everything else — degrees of freedom, contrasts, Tukey — is refinement of that one comparison.

The analysis workflow

The order of these steps is not a style preference; each step is only valid because the one before it passed. Doing them out of order is the single most common way to produce a wrong answer that looks right.

Lecture 1 · 1The probability setup

Lecture 1 is a toolbox lecture. Nothing in it is interesting on its own; every piece exists because ANOVA needs it later. It is worth knowing, for each item, which later result depends on it — that is noted throughout.

A probability setup has exactly three components, written together as the triple (Ω, ℱ, P):

ComponentSymbolWhat it is
Sample spaceΩEvery outcome the experiment could produce
EventsℱThe subsets of outcomes you can actually observe and assign probability to
Probability measurePThe rule assigning each event a number in [0, 1]

Kolmogorov's three axioms (1933) are the minimum rules P must obey:

  1. P(Ω) = 1 — something certainly happens.
  2. P(A) ≥ 0 for every event — probabilities are never negative.
  3. Countable additivity: for disjoint events A₁, A₂, …,  P(⋃ᵢ Aᵢ) = Σᵢ P(Aᵢ).
Why it works

Axiom 3 is the one that does real work later. Additivity over disjoint pieces is precisely what lets a total sum of squares split into non-overlapping parts. When lecture 4 claims orthogonal contrasts partition SSTr, it is the same structural idea: carve a whole into disjoint pieces and the pieces add back to the whole.

Lecture 1 · 2Random variables, PMFs and PDFs

A random variable is a function that assigns a number to each outcome. Heads → 1, tails → 0. The point is purely practical: you cannot average "heads", but you can average 1s and 0s.

Discrete

Countably many values. Described by a probability mass function p(y) = P(Y = y), with Σ p(y) = 1.

CDF: F(y) = P(Y ≤ y) = Σx ≤ y p(x).

Continuous

Values fill an interval. Described by a probability density function f(y) ≥ 0, with ∫ f(y) dy = 1.

CDF: F(y) = P(Y ≤ y) = ∫−∞y f(x) dx.

Why P(Y = 172.5) = 0 for a continuous variable

This is the checkpoint question on the slides, and it genuinely confuses people because it sounds false — someone is 172.5 cm tall.

Why it works

For a continuous variable, probability is area under the density, and a single point is a line with zero width — zero area. Density is not probability: dnorm(172.5, 172, 8) returns about 0.0499, which is a height, not a probability, and heights can exceed 1 for a narrow distribution. Probability only appears once you integrate over an interval. So P(168 ≤ Y ≤ 180) is a real, positive number, while P(Y = 172.5) is exactly zero.

Trap

In R, dnorm gives density (a height), pnorm gives cumulative probability (an area). Reaching for dnorm when you want a p-value is a classic slip. Remember the four prefixes: density · probability (area to the left) · quantile (the inverse of p) · random draws.

Lecture 1 · 3Expectation and variance

E(Y) = μ = Σ y · p(y)   (discrete)   = ∫ y f(y) dy   (continuous) Var(Y) = σ² = E[(Y − μ)²] = Σ (y − μ)² · p(y)   or   ∫ (y − μ)² f(y) dy

Expectation is the balance point of the distribution — put the density on a beam and this is where it balances. Variance is the average squared distance from that balance point, so it measures spread. Because it squares, it comes out in squared units; the standard deviation σ = √Var(Y) brings you back to the original units, which is why you report σ but compute with σ².

Sample versions

ȳ = 1n Σt=1n yt       s² = 1n − 1 Σt=1n (yt − ȳ)²
Why n − 1 and not n

You are measuring spread around ȳ, but ȳ was itself computed from the same data, and it sits closer to the data than the true μ does — ȳ is the value that minimises the sum of squared deviations. So dividing by n systematically underestimates. Dividing by n − 1 corrects it exactly (Bessel's correction). The intuition that generalises: you spent one degree of freedom estimating the mean, so only n − 1 independent pieces of information about spread remain. This is the same accounting that gives dfE = N − a in ANOVA — there you estimate a group means, so you lose a.

Lecture 1 · 4Rules for linear combinations

These four lines look trivial and are the most heavily used result in the entire course. Every standard error in lectures 3 and 4 is derived from them.

Constants and scaling

E(c) = c  ·  E(cY) = c E(Y)

Var(c) = 0  ·  Var(cY) = c² Var(Y)

Sums of independent variables

E(Σ cᵢYᵢ) = Σ cᵢμᵢ

Var(Σ cᵢYᵢ) = Σ cᵢ²σᵢ²

The load-bearing idea

Variance scales with c squared, means scale with c. Double a variable and its variance quadruples. The cᵢ² in the variance rule is literally the Σcᵢ² you will see inside every contrast standard error in lecture 4 — it is not a new formula, it is this one.

The consequence that matters: the variance of a mean

Apply the rules to Ȳ = (1/n) Σ Yᵢ with all cᵢ = 1/n:

Var(Ȳ) = Σ 1n² σ² = n · σ²n² = σ²n

This is the reason more data helps. The spread of individual observations never shrinks — σ² is a property of the population. But the spread of the average shrinks as 1/n. This single fact drives power, confidence interval width, and why the piglet study fails and the plant study succeeds.

Lecture 1 · 5The four distributions and how they connect

The slides list four distributions as if they were four separate facts. They are not — three of them are built out of the normal, and knowing the construction tells you what each one is for.

Why t has fatter tails than the normal

In Z = (ȳ − μ)/(σ/√n) the denominator is a fixed constant. In T = (ȳ − μ)/(s/√n) the denominator is itself estimated and therefore random. You now have two sources of variation instead of one, so extreme values happen more often — fatter tails. With few degrees of freedom your variance estimate is poor and the tails are very fat (t0.025,3 = 3.182); with many they are barely distinguishable from normal (t0.025,36 = 2.028 against z = 1.960). As k → ∞, tk → N(0,1).

On the exam

Three identities are worth memorising outright: Z² = χ²₁,  t²ₖ = F1,k,  t∞ = N(0,1). Also note that Var(tk) = k/(k−2) only exists for k > 2 — with 1 or 2 df the t-distribution has no finite variance.

Lecture 2 · 1The completely randomized design

Before any mathematics, the design has to be right, because no analysis can rescue a badly designed experiment. A completely randomized design (CRD) means experimental units are assigned to treatments entirely at random, with no other structure imposed.

The four design principles
PrincipleWhat it doesWhat breaks without it
RandomizationAssigns units to treatments by chanceSystematic differences between groups get mistaken for treatment effects
ReplicationMultiple units per treatmentYou have no way to estimate σ² at all — no SSE, no F-test
BlockingGroups similar units before randomizing withinKnown nuisance variation stays in the error term and hides the signal
BlindingHides the assignment from subject and/or assessorExpectation influences measurement

Randomization is what licenses the independence assumption. That is its statistical job — not fairness, but the fact that the errors then have no shared structure you failed to model.

Why balance matters

A design is balanced when every group has the same n. The piglet data has n = 2 in all three groups, so it is balanced. Three reasons this is worth arranging:

  • Maximum power for a given total N. Shown algebraically in L3.6 — it is not a rule of thumb, it falls out of minimising the variance of a difference.
  • Robustness. The F-test tolerates unequal variances far better when group sizes are equal.
  • Simpler algebra. With balance, the grand mean is just the average of the group means: μ = (1/a) Σ μᵢ. Without balance it is a weighted average, and the neat identities stop holding.

Lecture 2 · 2The effects model

yit = μ + τi + εit      subject to   Σi=1a τi = 0,   εit ~ iid N(0, σ²)

Why the constraint Σ τᵢ = 0 is necessary

Why it works

Count the parameters: you have a group means to describe, but the model as written has 1 + a parameters (μ plus a taus). That is one too many — the model is over-parameterised. Add 5 to μ and subtract 5 from every τᵢ and every fitted value is identical, so the data can never tell those solutions apart. A constraint pins down one specific solution. Sum-to-zero is the choice that makes μ mean "the overall level" and each τᵢ mean "this group's deviation from that level" — the interpretation the course wants. R's default constraint is different, which is why L3.7 matters.

The equivalent means model

Absorbing μ + τi into a single group mean μi gives yit = μi + εit. Same model, different bookkeeping. Use the means model when you care about group levels; use the effects model when you care about how much the treatment moved things, which is usually the scientific question.

Lecture 2 · 4The sums of squares decomposition

Take one observation's total distance from the grand mean and split it at the group mean:

(yit − ȳ··) = (ȳi· − ȳ··) + (yit − ȳi·)

That is trivially true — the middle terms cancel. The non-obvious part is that it survives squaring and summing:

SST = SSTr + SSE
Why the cross-term vanishes

Squaring a sum normally leaves a cross-term 2Σ(ȳi· − ȳ··)(yit − ȳi·). Sum over t within a group: the first factor is constant inside the group and comes out, leaving Σt(yit − ȳi·) — the sum of deviations from their own mean, which is zero by construction. So the cross-term dies for every group, and the identity holds exactly. Geometrically the two components are orthogonal, which is the same fact stated in vector language.

Lecture 2 · 5The two extremes

The slides show these as separate examples. Seeing them side by side is what makes the decomposition click, because they are the two ends of a single scale.

Pure signal · SSE = 0

Every piglet weighs exactly its group mean: 82, 82 / 83, 83 / 84, 84.

SST = 4, SSTr = 4, SSE = 0.

All the red arrows in the opening figure collapse to nothing. 100% of variation is explained by diet, and F is infinite.

Pure noise · SSTr = 0

Every group has the identical pair 82, 84 — all three means are 83.

SST = 6, SSTr = 0, SSE = 6.

All the green arrows vanish. Diet explains exactly 0%, F = 0, and p = 1.

Trap

In the pure-noise case p = 1.00, and it is tempting to write "the diets have no effect". That is wrong. p = 1 means the data are perfectly consistent with H₀, not that H₀ is true. You have failed to reject; you have not proven the null. With n = 2 you had almost no chance of detecting anything in the first place.

Lecture 2 · 6Degrees of freedom, mean squares, and F

Sums of squares are not comparable as they stand — SSE is built from more numbers than SSTr and would win by sheer volume. Dividing each by its degrees of freedom converts it into an average, and averages are comparable.

Degrees of freedom, counted as independent pieces of information

ErrorN − aN observations, minus the a group means you estimated336

They add exactly as the sums of squares do: dfT = dfTr + dfE.

MSTr = SSTra − 1     MSE = SSEN − a     F = MSTrMSE
Why F is centred at 1 under H₀

Both mean squares estimate the same σ² when H₀ is true — MSE always does, and MSTr does too when there are no real treatment effects, because then the group means differ only by sampling noise. A ratio of two estimates of the same quantity sits near 1. When treatments do differ, MSTr picks up the real spread between means on top of the noise, so it inflates and F grows. This also explains why the test is upper-tailed only: real effects can only push F up, never down.

Lecture 2 · 7Assumptions and how to check them

The F-test's distribution is derived assuming three things. If they fail, the p-value is not the number you think it is.

AssumptionIn the barnHow to checkIf violated
IndependencePiglets don't influence each other's growthNot testable after the fact — it is bought by randomization at design timeNothing can fix it later; the whole analysis is compromised
Normality of errorsMeasurement errors follow a bell curveQQ plot of residuals; shapiro.test(residuals(m))t-tests fairly robust; F loses power. Transform, or Kruskal–Wallis
Equal varianceAll diets give similarly spread weightsResiduals-vs-fitted (look for a funnel); bartlett.test()Standard errors wrong, CIs misleading. Transform, or weighted least squares
The load-bearing idea

Normality is assumed of the errors, not of the raw data. The observations are not expected to look normal — if the treatments work, the data are a mixture of several different means, which will look multi-humped. Always run the diagnostics on residuals(model).

Both datasets pass

# Piglets
shapiro.test(residuals(m1))    W = 0.8326,  p = 0.113
bartlett.test(Weight ~ Diet, data = SoyaData)   K² = 0.362, p = 0.834

# Trout
shapiro.test(residuals(model))  W = 0.9827,  p = 0.787
bartlett.test(Hemo ~ Sulfamerazine, data = Trout)  K² = 3.683, p = 0.298
Trap

These are the one place where a large p-value is what you want — here H₀ is "the assumption holds". Also note the piglet Shapiro test has almost no power with 6 observations; passing it is close to meaningless. Small samples pass assumption tests because the tests cannot see anything, not because the assumptions are met.

Lecture 2 · 8Power — why the same design reaches opposite conclusions

The lecture puts the PlantGrowth case study immediately after the piglets for a reason. Both are one-way CRDs with three groups. The only meaningful difference is n.

StudyanNdfEFpConclusion
Piglets32630.3330.7401Fail to reject
PlantGrowth31030274.8460.0159Reject H₀

Power is the probability of rejecting H₀ when it is genuinely false — 1 − β. It rises with:

  • Sample size n — because Var(ȳ) = σ²/n, so bigger n makes group means more precise and real gaps easier to see.
  • Effect size — larger true differences are easier to detect.
  • Lower σ² — less noise to see through. Blocking and tight experimental control both work by shrinking σ².
On the exam

Never write "the diets have no effect" after a non-significant F. The defensible sentence is: "There is no statistically significant evidence at α = 0.05 that diet affects weight gain (F2,3 = 0.33, p = 0.74). With n = 2 per group the study has very low power, so a real effect could easily have gone undetected." The second sentence is what separates a good answer from a merely correct one.

Lecture 3 · 1One statistic behind every test

Lecture 3 appears to introduce many different tests. It introduces one, applied to different quantities:

T = estimate − hypothesised valuestandard error of the estimate     with   df = N − a

The numerator is the size of the effect you think you found. The denominator is how much that estimate would bounce around from experiment to experiment. Their ratio asks: is this gap large relative to the uncertainty in measuring it?

The load-bearing idea

Every test in lectures 3 and 4 changes only the numerator. A group mean, the grand mean, a difference of two means, a contrast — same formula, same pooled MSE, same df = N − a. Learn the table of standard errors and you have learned all of it.

Every standard error in the course — all use pooled MSE with df = N − a
TargetEstimateStandard errorWhere

A difference μi − μjȳi· − ȳj·√MSE(1/ni + 1/nj)L3

Why pooled MSE and not the group's own variance

Why it works

You assumed all groups share the same σ². Under that assumption, each group's variance is an independent estimate of the same number, so averaging them all gives a far more stable estimate than using one group alone. Concretely for the piglets: NONS alone gives s² = 8 on 1 df; pooling all three gives MSE = 6 on 3 df. More degrees of freedom means a smaller critical value (3.182 instead of 12.706) and therefore more power. Pooling buys precision, and the equal-variance assumption is the price. That is why you check it.

Lecture 3 · 3Confidence intervals and their duality with tests

CI = estimate ± tα/2, N−a · se(estimate)

For the three piglet diets, with t0.025,3 · √3 = 3.1824 × 1.7321 = 5.512:

Those intervals are 11 kg wide on animals that weigh about 83 kg, and they overlap almost completely. The estimates are close to worthless — which is the honest summary of an experiment with two piglets per diet.

Duality — the shortcut worth knowing

A two-sided test at level α and a (1−α) confidence interval are the same procedure. Reject H₀: θ = θ₀ if and only if θ₀ falls outside the interval. So once you have the CI you never need to run the test — just look. This works in both directions and is frequently the fastest route to an exam answer.

Trap · what "95% confident" means

It is a statement about the procedure, not about this one interval. If you repeated the whole experiment many times, 95% of the intervals so constructed would contain the true μ. The true μ is a fixed number; it does not have a probability distribution. Saying "there is a 95% probability that μ is in [76.5, 87.5]" is the classic misstatement.

Lecture 3 · 4Inference on the error variance

So far σ² has been a nuisance parameter. Sometimes it is the quantity of interest — how noisy is this measurement process? The relevant sampling result is:

SSEσ² ~ χ²N−a

Rearranging for σ² gives an interval — but note carefully what happens to the direction:

CI for σ² = [ SSEχ²1−α/2, dfE , SSEχ²α/2, dfE ]

For the piglets, SSE = 18 on dfE = 3:

qchisq(c(0.975, 0.025), df = 3)      9.348404   0.2157953
18 / qchisq(c(0.975, 0.025), df = 3) 1.925462  83.412390

So the 95% CI for σ² is [1.93, 83.41]. Testing H₀: σ² = 1 by duality: 1 is outside the interval, so reject. Testing H₀: σ² = 20: inside, so fail to reject. The interval spans a factor of forty — with 3 degrees of freedom you know almost nothing about σ².

Trap

The slide gives the upper bound as 81.8, from rounding χ²0.025,3 to 0.22. The exact value 0.2157953 gives 83.41. If you work from a printed statistical table you will legitimately get ≈81.8 — say which you used. The lesson is that dividing by a small, heavily-rounded number amplifies the rounding error enormously.

The load-bearing idea

α is chosen by you, before seeing the data. β is a consequence — of your α, your sample size, the true effect size, and σ². You never observe β directly, which is exactly why a non-significant result is so easy to over-interpret.

Lecture 3 · 6Why balance maximises power

This is stated as a design principle in L2. Here is the algebra that makes it a theorem.

Var(ȳ1· − ȳ2·) = σ² ( 1n₁ + 1n₂ )

Hold the total N = n₁ + n₂ fixed and see what happens as you shift units between groups:

Allocating N = 22 units to two groups, variance in units of σ²
n₁n₂1/n₁ + 1/n₂Result
11110.1818Balanced — minimum
8140.19648% worse
4180.305668% worse
2200.5500Three times worse
Why it works

The reciprocal 1/n falls steeply for small n and flattens for large n. So moving one unit out of a small group costs far more than the same unit gains when added to a large one. The small group dominates the uncertainty of the comparison, and the twentieth observation in a group of twenty adds almost nothing. Formally, 1/n₁ + 1/n₂ subject to n₁ + n₂ = N is minimised at n₁ = n₂ = N/2.

Trap · the one that silently changes your answer

R's factory default is contr.treatment, not contr.sum. Under the default, (Intercept) is the first group's mean (82, not 83) and each coefficient is a difference from that baseline group, not a deviation from the grand mean. The ANOVA table, F-statistic and p-values are identical either way — only the coefficients change meaning. So this fails silently: you get plausible numbers that answer a different question. Set the option at the top of every script.

Two more details: R numbers the levels alphabetically (NONS, S1, S2 → Diet1, Diet2, Diet3), and under contr.sum the last level is the one omitted from the output.

The bridge to lecture 4

Testing one τi = 0 is a one-degree-of-freedom question carved out of the global F-test, and the two agree exactly:

T²N−a = F1, N−a

Both use the same MSE and the same dfE = N − a, and produce identical two-sided p-values. Lecture 4 turns this from a curiosity into the main tool.

Lecture 4 · 1What a contrast is

The F-test tells you there is a fire but not which room is burning. A contrast is how you ask a specific question.

Γ = Σi=1a ci μi      subject to   Σi=1a ci = 0
Why the coefficients must sum to zero

Substitute μi = μ + τi:

Γ = Σ ci(μ + τi) = μ Σ ci + Σ ciτi

When Σ ci = 0 the first term vanishes and Γ = Σ ciτi — a pure statement about treatment effects, with the baseline μ algebraically eliminated. Add 100 to every group mean and Γ is unchanged. That is what makes a contrast a comparison rather than a level.

Lecture 4 · 2Estimating and testing a contrast

The estimate replaces each unknown μi with its sample mean, and the variance comes straight from the L1.4 rules:

Γ̂ = Σ ci ȳi·     V(Γ̂) = Σ ci² · σ²n = σ²n Σ ci²     se(Γ̂) = √MSEn Σ ci²

That Σci² is literally the Σci²σi² rule from lecture 1 — nothing new has been introduced.

Lecture 4 · 3Orthogonality and the partition theorem

Two contrasts are orthogonal — mathematically independent, carrying non-overlapping information — when

Σi=1a c1i c2i = 0

Check the three trout contrasts pairwise:

PairElement-by-element productsSum
c₁ · c₂(1)(0) + (−1)(0) + (0)(1) + (0)(−1)0orthogonal
c₁ · c₃(1)(1) + (−1)(1) + (0)(−1) + (0)(−1)0orthogonal
c₂ · c₃(0)(1) + (0)(1) + (1)(−1) + (−1)(−1)0orthogonal

Each contrast has its own sum of squares, with exactly one degree of freedom because it asks exactly one question:

SSC = (Σ ci ȳi·)²1n Σ ci²     dfC = 1     MSC = SSC
The partition theorem

With a groups you can build exactly a − 1 mutually orthogonal contrasts — matching dfTr = a − 1 exactly, which is not a coincidence. When you do, their sums of squares add up to the treatment sum of squares: SSTr = Σj SSCj. The omnibus test has been dissected into a−1 independent one-df questions with nothing left over and nothing double-counted.

Trap · the slide's split numbers are wrong

The lecture 4 slide states the partition as 11.10 + 12.79 + 2.91 = 26.80. Those three values do sum correctly, but they are not what the stated contrasts produce. Running the code on the slide's own page gives 22.685 + 0.578 + 3.540. Trust the R output — and note that the difference matters scientifically: the slide's version implies the two largest effects are comparable, while the real split shows one comparison dominating everything else.

Lecture 4 · 4Why T² = F

Square the contrast t-statistic and watch it turn into an F-statistic:

T₀² = ( Γ̂se(Γ̂) )² = (Σ ciȳi·)²MSEn Σ ci² = SSCMSE = F₀

Verify numerically on the trout contrast: T₀ = −3.8028, so T₀² = 14.461. And SSC/MSE = 22.685/1.5686 = 14.461. Identical, and both give p = 0.000534.

The load-bearing idea

A t-test is an F-test with one numerator degree of freedom. This is why you may test a contrast either way, why the split= output in R agrees with a hand t-test, and why the same tables serve both. The only asymmetry: t is signed, so it tells you the direction; F is squared and does not.

Lecture 4 · 5The multiple testing problem

With a groups there are m = a(a−1)/2 pairwise comparisons. Test each at α = 0.05 and, treating the tests as independent:

FWER = P(at least one false positive) = 1 − (1 − α)m
The family-wise error rate compounds fast
Groups aTests mFWER at α = 0.05Meaning
3314.3%Already triple the nominal rate
4626.5%The trout study — a 1-in-4 chance of a false claim
51040.1%Coin-flip territory
104590.1%A false discovery is now near-certain
Why it compounds

Each test has a 95% chance of behaving. Run m independent tests and the chance that all behave is 0.95m, which decays geometrically. As a grows linearly, m grows quadratically and the error rate compounds on top of that. This is a major documented cause of irreproducible published science, not a theoretical worry.

Trap · the independence assumption

The formula 1 − (1−α)m assumes the m tests are independent. Pairwise comparisons are not — they share group means (S05−S00 and S10−S00 both involve S00) and they share the same MSE. The true FWER is somewhat lower than the table. The formula is a useful bound and is what the exam expects, but it is an approximation and you should say so.

Lecture 4 · 6The three corrections

All three raise the bar for significance. They differ in what set of comparisons they protect — and that is what should drive your choice.

Bonferroni

α/m

Test each comparison at α/m instead of α. For a = 4: 0.05/6 = 0.0083.

Protects: any m comparisons you named in advance.

Use when m is small and every comparison was planned. Gets brutal fast as m grows.

Tukey HSD

qα,a,N−a

Uses the studentized range distribution — the distribution of (ȳmax − ȳmin)/se.

Protects: all a(a−1)/2 pairwise comparisons, exactly.

Use when you want all pairs. Highest power for that job — the default choice.

Scheffé

√(a−1)F

Threshold S = √(a−1) Fα,a−1,N−a.

Protects: every possible contrast, including ones you invented after seeing the data.

Use when you went looking. Broadest protection, lowest power.

Why Tukey beats Bonferroni for pairwise work

Bonferroni treats the m comparisons as unrelated and pays full price for each. Tukey knows something Bonferroni doesn't: if the largest gap between any two means is not significant, no other gap can be either. So it only needs to control the distribution of that single largest range, rather than bounding all m tests separately. Modelling the actual worst case instead of assuming the worst possible case is what buys back the power.

All four thresholds on one scale

Tukey's decision rule

|ȳi· − ȳj·| > qα, a, N−a √MSEn    the right-hand side is the HSD

For the trout: q0.05,4,36 = 3.8088 and √1.5686/10 = 0.3961, so

HSD = 3.8088 × 0.3961 = 1.5085

Any two group means differing by more than 1.51 g/100 ml are significantly different.

Trap

Each Tukey interval is a simultaneous interval — all six together have 95% coverage, which is why they are wider than six separate 95% intervals would be. Do not compare a Tukey interval against an ordinary t-interval and conclude R made an error.

Unbalanced data

When ni ≠ nj, R silently switches to the Tukey–Kramer adjustment, replacing √MSE/n with √(MSE/2)(1/ni + 1/nj). Each pair then gets its own margin. Nothing needs changing in your code — but know it happened.

The traps, collected

#TrapWhat actually happens
1Grouping column left as text or numbersR fits a regression on a numeric predictor, or errors on text. Wrong df, no warning. Always as.factor().
2read.csv instead of read.csv2European decimal commas parse as text; numeric columns silently become character.
3Forgetting contr.sumCoefficients silently mean "difference from the first group" instead of "deviation from grand mean". F and p unaffected, so nothing looks wrong.
4Pairwise tests before the omnibus F rejectsFishing. The FWER is uncontrolled and the result is not defensible.
5Reading a large p as "no effect"It means "no evidence", which with small n usually means low power.
6Using one group's variance instead of pooled MSEFewer df, larger critical value, less power — and it contradicts the equal-variance assumption you already made.
7Swapping the χ² CI boundsThe large quantile gives the lower bound, because SSE is divided by it.
8Forgetting the n in SSTrEach squared group deviation counts once per observation. Off by a factor of n.
9Rounding intermediate valuesRounding T to 2 decimals before a tail probability can flip a borderline decision. Round last.
10Checking normality of the raw dataThe assumption is about the errors. Use residuals(m).
11R's sd vs variance in dnorm/pnorm/qnormThese take the standard deviation, not σ². Passing a variance silently gives wrong probabilities.
12Comparing a Tukey interval to a plain t-intervalTukey intervals are simultaneous and therefore wider by design.

On the exam

If you compute by hand from printed statistical tables, your answers will differ from R in the third decimal. That is expected and fine — state which you used. What is not fine is rounding midway through a calculation, which is what produces the two p-value discrepancies above.
